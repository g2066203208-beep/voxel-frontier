2.2.2. Tower parameters
As illustrated in Fig. 3, the SCHT is a steel–concrete hybrid tower
consisting of a steel upper section and a concrete lower section, with a
total height of 158 m. The concrete section extends from 0 m to 112 m,
while the remaining portion is composed of steel. The tower of the
SCHT wind turbine is modeled as a typical cantilever beam structure,
with its cross-sectional geometric characteristics varying along the
height of the tower.
For the concrete tower, the lower two sections utilize C70 concrete
as per Chinese standards, while the remaining sections use C65 con-
crete. The reinforcement for the concrete sections employs HRB335
steel bars. Additionally, the prestressed tendons are made of externally
unbonded, high-strength, low-relaxation wire ropes with a diameter of
15.2 mm and an initial prestress of 1280 MPa. For the steel sections
of the tower, Q345 steel is used. Detailed material parameters are

2.3. Wind loads
During operation, an OWT with SCHT relies on its blades to harness
wind energy, bearing the majority of the wind loads and transferring
these loads downwards. This section will delve into the theoretical
calculations of the wind loads experienced by an OWT with SCHT. As is
commonly understood, wind speed comprises both mean and turbulent
components [30], denoted by 𝑣(𝑡) =̄ 𝑣+̃ 𝑣(𝑡). The mean wind speed at
a given height 𝑧is determined through a power-law profile, whereas
the turbulent component of wind speed is calculated using the spectral
representation method. For the latter, the Kaimal spectrum is utilized
as the reference spectrum [31].
As illustrated in Fig. 4(a), the program calculates the wind speed at
each point in a 31 × 31 grid. This generates a stochastic wind field,
which is then applied to the blades and tower structures to assess
the effects of turbulent wind-induced loadings on the wind turbine.
Fig. 4(b) depicts the time history of wind speed at a specific point in
the turbulent wind field.
The Blade Element Momentum (BEM) theory [28,32,33] is then
employed to approximate the load effects acting on the blades. This
method offers high computational efficiency compared to computa-
tional fluid dynamics, while also maintaining a high level of accuracy.
BEM theory combines blade element theory and momentum theory to
determine the normal forces 𝑃N and tangential forces 𝑃T acting on
blade elements, based on the aerodynamic characteristics of the blades
and the incoming wind speed profiles. Recognizing that direct iteration
methods exhibit poor stability, this paper adopts the computational
approach outlined in [34,35] to transform the traditional two-variable
iterative BEM equations into single-variable equations with unknown
inflow angles, employing stable and reliable root-finding algorithms
to solve them. After obtaining the aerodynamic loads, specifically the
normal forces 𝑃N and tangential forces 𝑃T, through the BEM theory,
the generalized aerodynamic loads acting on each degree of freedom
are calculated using the principle of virtual work. The virtual work
generated by the aerodynamic loads is expressed as follows [33]:
